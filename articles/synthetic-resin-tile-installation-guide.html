Synthetic Resin Tile Installation Guide: Purlins, Roof Slope and Fixing Screws

By Henan Wudi New Materials Co., Ltd. · November 2026 · 7 min read

A good ASA synthetic resin tile can be installed badly — and when that happens, the distributor is the one who gets the phone call. In our experience, most complaints about "leaking" or "noisy" resin roofs trace back not to the sheet itself but to three installation variables: roof slope, purlin spacing, and how the screws were driven. None of them is difficult to get right, but every one of them is easy to get wrong on a busy job site.

This guide explains the installation fundamentals in plain terms, so you can brief your contractors, include the right notes in your own installation sheet, and avoid the most common callbacks. It is written for importers and distributors who sell resin tiles but do not necessarily install roofs themselves.

1. Start with the slope: 9.5–20 degrees is the working range

Synthetic resin tiles are a water-shedding roof covering, not a waterproofing membrane. They rely on the roof pitch to drain rain quickly into the gutter system. The widely used working range for corrugated resin tiles is a slope of 9.5° to 20° (roughly a 1.7:12 to 4.5:12 pitch):

  • Below about 9.5°, water drains slowly, lap joints stay wet for long periods, and capillary creep under the overlap becomes a real leak risk. Flat conversions should be re-structured to reach this minimum pitch — which is exactly why resin tiles are popular for flat-to-slope roof renovation projects.
  • Between 9.5° and 20°, drainage is fast, laps stay dry, and the sheet self-weight keeps the roof plane stable. This range suits the vast majority of residential and light commercial roofs.
  • Above 20°, the roof drains well but the fixing load at the lower edge increases in high-wind areas, so screw density and edge fixing deserve extra attention.

If a customer asks "can I put this on a nearly flat roof?", the honest answer is: not without changing the structure to create pitch. Selling a flat roof an unmodified resin tile is the fastest way to earn a bad reputation.

2. Purlins: spacing follows thickness, and material matters

Resin tiles are fixed to purlins (battens), not directly to a solid deck. The purlin material is flexible — hot-dip galvanized steel, treated timber, or aluminum — but spacing must match the sheet thickness and profile. Typical layouts look like this:

Tile thicknessTypical purlin spacingNotes
2.0mm≈ 60–66 cmKeep to the tighter end in high-wind zones and where roofers walk on the sheets during fixing.
2.5mm≈ 66–75 cmThe mainstream export thickness; a comfortable default for residential spans.
3.0mm≈ 75–90 cmHigher rigidity allows wider spans; verify with the supplier for the exact profile model.

These figures are planning references — the exact recommendation depends on the profile model (880mm, 1050mm, 900mm or 1035mm effective width), the project's wind and snow conditions, and the purlin section used. Always confirm the spacing for the specific model and thickness you import, and pass it on to your contractors in writing. Two purlin details that prevent problems later:

  • Level and aligned purlins. A resin tile will follow the plane of the purlins. If one purlin sits 5 mm proud, the roof shows a visible hump — and the screws at that line take uneven load.
  • An edge purlin under each roof edge and eave. The front edge of each sheet and the eave line need support so fixings bite into structure, not air.

3. Fixing screws: the single most common source of leaks

Resin tiles are fixed with self-tapping screws fitted with an EPDM sealing washer. The screws go at the wave crest — the highest point of the corrugation — never in the valley, where water flows. Get this one detail right and you have eliminated the majority of potential leak points.

  • Drill pilot holes slightly larger than the screw shank (typically 2–3 mm oversize at the crest) so the sheet can expand and contract with temperature without stressing the fixing. Resin tiles move more with heat than metal sheets; a rigid fixing is a cracked fixing.
  • Drive screws square and stop at the right torque. Over-driven screws crush the EPDM washer and deform the wave crest; under-driven screws leave the washer loose. The washer should be visibly compressed against the crest — flat, not wrinkled, not squashed thin.
  • Use the right screw length so the thread engages the purlin adequately — as a rule, at least 20–25 mm of thread into steel or timber beyond the sheet.
  • Fix every wave crest at the eaves, ridge and side laps, and roughly every second or third crest in the field of the sheet, per the model's fixing plan.

On hot climates, check whether local contractors use standard EPDM washers or higher-temperature grades — cheap rubber washers harden and crack within a couple of seasons of intense UV, and that becomes a leak that looks like a "product defect". A quality washer costs a few cents and protects the whole reputation of the roof.

4. Overlaps and direction of laying

Laying direction is dictated by the prevailing wind and rain: start from the leeward end so that the side laps face away from the driving rain. Standard practice:

  • Side overlap: one full wave overlap (aligned with the profile's effective width), with the lap line over a purlin so it can be screwed down.
  • End overlap: typically 10–20 cm depending on slope — more overlap at the shallower end of the slope range. The end lap must also land on a purlin.
  • Never cut laps short to save material. A few square meters of saving is worthless next to one leak callback.

Cutting is done with a fine-tooth hand saw or power saw with a metal-cutting blade; support the sheet flat while cutting so the wave does not crack. Hot-cut sparks from abrasive discs can scorch the ASA surface, so disc grinders should be avoided on the visible face.

5. Ridge, hip and flashing details

The main sheets handle the field of the roof; the accessories handle everything the water hits at a change of direction. Ridge caps, hip caps, eave trims and flashings are fixed with the same crest-and-EPDM principle, and the ridge should be capped so that water running down the top courses is directed over — not under — the last row of tiles. Where a resin tile meets a wall, use a proper flashing and sealant, and never rely on silicone alone across a wide gap.

A short checklist you can hand to your contractors

  • Slope within 9.5°–20°; do not install on near-flat decks.
  • Purlin spacing confirmed for the specific model and thickness; purlins level and aligned.
  • Screws at wave crests only, with EPDM washers, square-driven, correctly torqued.
  • Pilot holes oversize at crests to allow thermal movement.
  • Laps facing away from prevailing rain; side and end laps over purlins.
  • Ridge, hip and flashings fixed and sealed as a complete system.
Include an installation sheet with every container. The cheapest warranty you will ever buy is a one-page printed fixing plan in the local language, packed on top of the first bundle. It sets the contractor's expectations before the first screw is driven — and it protects you when a leak turns out to be a workmanship issue rather than a product one.

Quality installation and quality product go together: the fixing plan only works if the sheet itself holds its shape and color, which is why we always advise buyers to verify the material before the order, using the questions in our supplier checklist.
