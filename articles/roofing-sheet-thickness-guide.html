How to Choose Roofing Sheet Thickness: 2.0mm vs 2.5mm vs 3.0mm — A Practical Guide for Importers

By Henan Wudi New Materials Co., Ltd. · November 2026 · 6 min read

When distributors first request a quotation for ASA synthetic resin tiles, the question that decides the whole commercial conversation is usually not color or profile — it is thickness. Thickness drives the price per square meter, the weight of your container, the purlin spacing your customers can use, and ultimately which projects you can sell into. Choose wrong and you either lose the quote to a cheaper competitor or win it with a product that disappoints in the field.

This guide explains, from a factory perspective, what actually changes between 2.0mm, 2.5mm and 3.0mm tiles — and how to match thickness to the market you sell into.

First: what "thickness" actually means on a resin tile

A quality ASA synthetic resin tile is a co-extruded multilayer sheet: an ASA surface layer for weather and color stability, and a PVC/ASA composite structure underneath for rigidity. The total thickness is measured across the full wave section of the tile, including the corrugations — not just the flat top. When you compare quotes from different suppliers, always confirm that the thickness being quoted is total thickness across the wave, because a "2.5mm" tile measured only on the flat crest is a significantly thinner (and cheaper to make) product.

Our production range covers 1.8mm up to 5.0mm depending on model, but for most export markets the practical buying decision comes down to three points: 2.0mm, 2.5mm and 3.0mm.

The three common thicknesses, compared

ThicknessTypical position in the marketWhat it means for your customers
2.0mmEntry / price-driven segmentLightest and lowest cost per m². Works for budget housing, temporary structures and price-sensitive rural projects where roof spans are short.
2.5mmMainstream export choiceThe volume segment. Better rigidity and a noticeably more substantial feel in hand; suitable for most residential and light commercial roofs with standard purlin spacing.
3.0mmPremium / demanding applicationsHighest rigidity and impact resistance of the three. Chosen for villa projects, resorts, public buildings and areas with strong wind or heavy seasonal rain.

One important honest note: thickness alone does not equal quality. A well-made 2.0mm tile with a full ASA surface layer will outlast a poorly made 3.0mm tile with a reduced ASA layer. Thickness is a strength and rigidity decision; the surface layer composition is a lifespan decision. You need to get both right, and you should ask any supplier about both separately (see our supplier due-diligence checklist).

How thickness affects price per square meter

Resin tiles are sold by the square meter, and material cost scales roughly with the volume of raw material in the sheet — so stepping from 2.0mm to 2.5mm adds meaningful cost per m², and 2.5mm to 3.0mm again. The exact FOB or CIF price depends on the profile (880mm, 1050mm, 900mm or 1035mm effective width models have different material usage), color, and current raw material prices.

For your own margin planning, the useful framing is this: the price difference between thicknesses is smaller than the difference in what customers will pay for them. A 2.5mm or 3.0mm tile lets you position your product line at a higher tier in your local market, and the premium your customers accept for a "heavy-duty" tile is usually larger than the extra factory cost. Many distributors import 2.0mm as a price anchor and 2.5–3.0mm as their profit line.

How thickness affects purlin spacing and structure

Thicker sheets are more rigid, which gives your engineering customers more flexibility:

  • Thinner sheets (2.0mm) generally want closer purlin spacing to keep the roof plane flat and prevent flexing under foot traffic during installation.
  • Mid thickness (2.5mm) handles standard residential purlin layouts comfortably and is the safest default for a mixed product range.
  • Thicker sheets (3.0mm) tolerate wider spans and give a stiffer walkable feel on the roof — valued by contractors on larger commercial jobs.

Exact recommended purlin spacing depends on the profile model and the project's wind and snow conditions, so the right practice is to ask your supplier for the installation recommendation for the specific model and thickness you are importing — and to pass it on to your customers in your own installation sheet. That one document prevents a large share of the complaints that distributors receive.

How thickness affects your container

Thickness also changes your shipping economics. Thicker tiles weigh more per square meter and, depending on packing, can slightly reduce how many square meters fit per container. As a rough planning frame, a 40HQ container of 2.5mm tile carries fewer square meters than the same container of 2.0mm tile, and the freight cost per m² rises accordingly. Container capacity varies with model, thickness and packing method, so treat any number as an estimate — contact us for an exact loading plan for your specific mix of models, thicknesses and colors before you calculate landed cost.

So which thickness should you import?

The decision reduces to three questions about your market:

  • What do your competitors sell? If the local market is anchored at thin, cheap sheets, a 2.5mm offering with a genuine ASA layer is a strong differentiation story at a modest premium.
  • What projects dominate? Budget rural housing → 2.0mm. Mainstream residential and commercial → 2.5mm. Villas, resorts, coastal and high-wind regions → 3.0mm.
  • How will you position your brand? If you are building a quality brand rather than competing on price alone, do not lead with the thinnest sheet. One complaint about a flexing roof costs more margin than a year of price differences.

A common and practical approach for a new distributor: start with a mixed container — 2.5mm as the core stock, a smaller quantity of 3.0mm for premium inquiries, and optionally some 2.0mm to defend the low end of the market. This lets you quote almost any project from one container without over-committing capital to a thickness you have not yet sold.
